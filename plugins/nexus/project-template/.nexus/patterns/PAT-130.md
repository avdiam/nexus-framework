# PAT-130: Source-Fix Over Symptom-Machinery
*Version: 1.0.0 | Date: 2026-06-18 | Sprint: 105*

## Metadata
- **Type**: principle
- **Status**: active
- **Created**: 2026-06-18

## Problem
When a symptom recurs — a cost that keeps growing, a rule that keeps getting skipped, an ambiguity that keeps needing adjudication — the LLM's default reflex is to build machinery *around* the symptom: optimize the loader, add a governing rule, write a compensating bridge. That machinery then becomes a permanent maintenance surface that must be carried, versioned, and reasoned about at every future instance. The cheaper and more honest fix is usually at the **source**: change the structure that *produces* the symptom so that the symptom — and the machinery that would handle it — stops existing. The reflex misfires because the felt pain is at the symptom (the slow load, the missed gate, the contested branch), so attention lands there rather than at the producing structure.

## Context
- **Use When**:
  - A recurring cost, skip, or ambiguity tempts you to add optimization, enforcement, or bridging machinery
  - The source structure (artifact size, branch existence, concern bundling) is itself editable AND is what generates the symptom
  - The machinery would need maintaining at every future instance, not just the current one
  - You catch yourself optimizing *how* something is loaded/handled before asking whether the something needs to be that large/branchy/bundled at all
- **Not When**:
  - The source is fixed or external and only the symptom is within your control (then symptom-handling is the only lever)
  - The symptom is genuinely one-off — machinery cost is lower than the restructure cost and won't recur
  - Restructuring the source is Major-magnitude and a bounded, local handler is honestly cheaper for now (a scope decision, not a reflex)
- **Prerequisites**: The producing structure is identifiable and editable within the current scope.

## Solution
Before optimizing, governing, or bridging a recurring symptom, ask: *"What source structure produces this symptom, and can I change that instead?"* Prefer the structural source-fix when it eliminates the symptom **class** rather than managing each instance. Name the producing structure explicitly, propose the source change, and only fall back to symptom-machinery when the source is out of scope or the restructure is genuinely more expensive than the machinery it would replace.

### Implementation
- **Detection cue**: you are about to add an optimizer, a governing rule, an enforcement check, or a bridge/cross-reference to cope with something that recurs. That impulse IS the cue to look one level up at the source.
- **Move**: locate the producing structure → ask whether shrinking / removing / unbundling it eliminates the symptom → cost-compare source-fix (paid once) vs machinery (paid every instance).

### Variations
- **Variant A (cost / load)**: shrink the source artifact before optimizing how it loads. A smaller source needs no loader optimization. *(Origin: ISS-220 → ISS-223 "reduce the source before optimizing the load.")*
- **Variant B (lifecycle / design)**: eliminate the ambiguous branch at the source rather than adding governing rules to adjudicate it. A branch that cannot be reached needs no governance. *(Origin: ISS-222 "eliminate the ambiguous branch over governing it.")*

## Rationale
- **Theoretical Basis**: Machinery around a symptom is cost paid *forever* — it must be maintained, versioned, and reasoned about at every instance the symptom recurs. A source-fix is cost paid *once*; it removes the entire instance-class, so there is nothing left to maintain. Optimizing/governing/bridging implicitly treats the symptom as fixed and the structure as given — but the structure is precisely what is under your control. The asymmetry favors the source even when only one instance exists today, because the source-fix also covers every future instance for free.
- **Empirical Evidence**: Sprint 105 — ISS-220→223 (shrink the source corpus rather than optimize its load path) and ISS-222 (remove the ambiguous lifecycle branch rather than add a governing rule to adjudicate it). Two independent domains reaching the same structural move.
- **Design Decisions**: Named "Over Symptom-Machinery" rather than "Always Source-Fix" because legitimate not-when cases exist (fixed/external source, genuine one-offs, Major-magnitude restructures). The pattern guides where to *look first*, not a mandate.

## Anti-Patterns
- **Anti-Pattern 1: Optimize the loader, never question the load**
  - **Why Wrong**: Tuning how a bloated artifact is fetched/parsed accepts the bloat as permanent and pays optimization complexity forever; shrinking the artifact would have made the optimizer unnecessary.
  - **Example**: A reference corpus is slow to load, so a caching/lazy-load layer is added — when half the corpus was redundant and could have been deleted.
- **Anti-Pattern 2: Govern the ambiguity instead of removing it**
  - **Why Wrong**: Adding rules, gates, and adjudication prose to manage a branch that shouldn't exist multiplies the surface that can drift; deleting the branch removes the need for all of it.
  - **Example**: Two near-identical lifecycle paths keep getting confused, so a disambiguation rule is written — when merging or deleting one path removes the confusion at the source.
- **Anti-Pattern 3: Source-fix when the source is out of scope**
  - **Why Wrong**: Forcing a structural rewrite of something fixed/external (or Major-magnitude) under the banner of "fix the source" is over-engineering; here the bounded symptom-handler is the honest choice.
  - **Example**: Rewriting a third-party schema you don't own to avoid a one-line local adapter.

## Consequences
- **Benefits**: Eliminates the whole symptom-class rather than one instance; removes permanent maintenance surface; keeps the system smaller and the structure honest; covers all future instances for free.
- **Tradeoffs**: Requires the analytical step of identifying the producing structure (sometimes non-obvious); the source-fix may touch a more central artifact than the local handler would.
- **Risks**: Over-applying — forcing structural rewrites on fixed/external sources or genuine one-offs where a bounded handler is cheaper; mistaking a scope decision (Major restructure) for a reflex source-fix.

## Examples

### Before/After Comparison
**Without Pattern**: A growing reference corpus loads slowly; the instinct is to add a lazy-loading/caching layer (machinery) so the slow load hurts less — the corpus stays bloated and the loader complexity is now permanent.
**With Pattern**: First ask what produces the slow load — the corpus size. Shrink the source corpus (remove redundancy, consolidate). The load is fast with *no* loader machinery to maintain. (ISS-220→223.)

### Application Example
ISS-222 (Sprint 105): an ambiguous lifecycle branch kept requiring adjudication. The considered fix was a governing rule to decide the branch each time (machinery). The chosen fix eliminated the ambiguous branch at the source, so no governing rule was needed — the symptom-class disappeared rather than being managed.

## Validation (Optional)
- **Success Indicator**: After resolution, there is *less* structure than before (an artifact shrank, a branch/rule was removed) and no new permanent handler was added.
- **Edge Cases**: External/fixed sources and Major-magnitude restructures legitimately fall to symptom-handling — the pattern guides where to look first, not an absolute.

## Notes and Observations
The two originating instances came from independent domains (load-cost optimization and lifecycle-branch design) yet converged on the identical move — strong signal the wisdom is the structural meta-principle, not either domain-specific tactic. Relationship to PAT-103 is parent→instance: PAT-103 owns the sharp, well-grounded adversarial-finding trigger ("rule X lists A,B,C but not D"); PAT-130 generalizes the "act at the source, not the symptom" move across cost, lifecycle, and bundling. Keeping them separate preserves PAT-103's actionable specificity while capturing the broader reflex.
