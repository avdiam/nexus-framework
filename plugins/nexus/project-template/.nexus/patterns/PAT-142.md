# PAT-142: Bound the Audit Predicate in Both Directions
*Version: 1.0.0 | Date: 2026-08-25 | Sprint: 110*

## Metadata
- **Type**: methodology
- **Status**: active
- **Created**: 2026-08-25

## Problem

A mechanical audit over a corpus is only ever as good as the predicate its author wrote, and that predicate can be wrong in two opposite directions at once.

**Too narrow.** A detector list is a fixed vocabulary of flagged tokens. A claim can be completely false while using none of them, so an entire class of defect is *structurally unreachable* — not missed by accident, but outside what the instrument can express. The class that matters most is usually the one that reads as ordinary prose.

**Too broad.** An acceptance condition of the form "grep for `{token}` returns 0" silently asserts that every occurrence of the token is an instance of the thing being removed. The moment one live construct legitimately shares that spelling, the condition becomes *unsatisfiable by correct work* — and the only way to satisfy it literally is to edit correct files into wrong ones.

Both failures are invisible at authoring time, because the predicate reads rigorous. Both are invisible at run time, because a clean corpus and a corrupted one produce identical output.

## Context

- **Use When**:
  - Authoring a token-based sweep over a corpus — dead-code removal, deprecated-API purges, secret scanning, banned-word checks, dependency removal, documentation truth-passes
  - Writing the exit criterion or success criterion for any such audit, especially one phrased as a count reaching zero
  - Designing a checklist that mixes grep-shaped detectors with judgment-shaped ones
  - Reviewing an audit that reported a clean result over a corpus nobody believes is clean
- **Not When**:
  - The token set is genuinely closed and provably has no live homonyms (a generated identifier, a UUID, a versioned symbol nobody else can spell)
  - The audit is exploratory orientation with no decision hanging on completeness
  - The corpus is small enough to read end-to-end, where the predicate is a convenience rather than the bound
- **Prerequisites**: The corpus is greppable and the flagged tokens can be enumerated before the run.

## Solution

Before running the audit, produce **two enumerations** and write the acceptance condition against both.

**1. Enumerate what no token can reach.** Ask what a false claim in this corpus would look like if it used none of your flagged vocabulary. Each such class gets a judgment-shaped companion detector, and that detector needs its own closing step — *never the grep predicate*, which cannot see it. Scope the companion to the **whole artifact**, not to the section where the class was first found: a false claim of this kind is typically asserted more than once, and the second assertion sits outside the section its topic belongs to.

**2. Enumerate the live homonyms.** For each flagged token, list the live constructs that legitimately share its spelling. Then phrase the acceptance condition as **"every hit is fixed or classified"**, never "zero hits". A classification column costs one line per exception and makes the correct-by-scoping cases visible instead of adversarial.

**3. Prove the checker consumed something.** A judgment-shaped closing step needs a countable receipt — the number of rule-sentences examined per file, the number of rows the parser actually read — so an incomplete sweep is visibly incomplete rather than silently complete. (See PAT-138 for the general instrument-audit discipline this specialises.)

### Implementation

1. Write the flagged-token list.
2. For each token, list the live constructs that share the spelling. If the list is non-empty, the predicate is `every hit fixed or classified`, not `count == 0`.
3. Write the claim-classes that use none of the tokens. For each, name a judgment-shaped detector and its whole-artifact scope.
4. Give every judgment-shaped detector a countable closing receipt.
5. Run. Record per-detector: hits found, hits fixed, hits classified-as-live, and rows/sentences consumed.
6. Accept only when every hit is dispositioned **and** every judgment detector's receipt is non-trivial.

### Quick Reference

| Predicate defect | Symptom | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Too narrow | Audit passes on a corpus with obvious false claims | No-grep companion detector, whole-artifact scope |
| Too broad | Exit criterion can only be met by editing correct files | "Every hit fixed or classified" + live-homonym allow-list |
| Vacuous | Zero findings, output identical to a real clean run | Assert rows/sentences consumed before trusting the zero |

## Rationale

- **Theoretical Basis**: An audit's coverage is bounded by its author's enumeration of what might be wrong, and its *safety* is bounded by the author's enumeration of what is legitimately right. A predicate is a claim about both, but it is written as though it were a claim about neither — which is why it reads rigorous while being wrong in either direction.
- **Empirical Evidence**: ISS-089 (Sprint 110) reproduced both halves repeatedly. **Narrow**: the no-grep class (D10) fired four times across four guides — `quick-start-guide.md` taught the retired pre-Control-Levels consent model, the exact class the issue existed to fix, and scored **zero on five detectors** because it used no retired token; two later reproductions came from isolated workers who had never seen the earlier cases, and one of them asserted a dead actor six times across three sections. **Broad**: two different detectors on two different surfaces collided with live homonyms in a single phase — step 5.2's condition against `mcp_tool` (a real Claude Code hook `type`) and `mcpServers` (a real subagent frontmatter field), which would have required editing four correct files into wrong ones; and SC-02's condition against `85%`, where three distinct quantities share the token and only two were stale, and against `{placeholder}`, where all twenty prose hits were intentional command-template parameters. **Vacuous**: two checkers returned false findings caught only because their magnitude was implausible — the inverse failure returns zero and reads exactly like a pass.
- **Design Decisions**: Both directions are held in one pattern because they are enumerated in the same sitting, by the same person, against the same token list — and because fixing only one produces an audit that is confidently wrong the other way. Splitting them would file one discipline under two triggers and surface neither at predicate-authoring time, which is the only moment either is cheap.

## Anti-Patterns

- **Anti-Pattern 1**: Writing the exit criterion as "grep returns 0".
  - **Why Wrong**: It asserts that no live construct shares the spelling — an assertion nobody checked. Where it is false, the criterion rewards corruption and punishes correct work.
  - **Example**: "The dead-token grep over the five live files returns 0" — satisfiable only by deleting real hook types and real frontmatter fields.
- **Anti-Pattern 2**: Treating a full detector sweep as coverage.
  - **Why Wrong**: Coverage of a vocabulary is not coverage of a claim-space. The most damaging claims are the ones stated in ordinary language.
  - **Example**: A guide passing five detectors and an all-zero closing predicate while teaching a retired consent model in plain prose.
- **Anti-Pattern 3**: Scoping a judgment-shaped detector to the section where its class was first found.
  - **Why Wrong**: A false behavioural claim is typically asserted more than once, and the repeat sits outside the section its topic belongs to. The section-scoped sweep completes and reads as complete.
  - **Example**: A dead actor fixed in Core-Concepts and left standing in a later section of the same file.
- **Anti-Pattern 4**: Manufacturing fixes to satisfy a predicate that turned out to be unsatisfiable.
  - **Why Wrong**: It inverts the audit — the corpus is degraded to make the instrument agree. The correct move is to amend the predicate at a gate and record the amendment.
  - **Example**: Archiving artifacts to hit an expected count after an honest test returned one.

## Relationships
- **Requires**: none
- **Enhances**: PAT-135 (evidence-artifact-proves-execution-not-adequacy), PAT-138 (check-the-instrument-before-the-reading), PAT-125 (negative-space-verification)
- **Conflicts**: none
- **Alternatives**: PAT-138 audits an instrument *after* a suspicious reading; this pattern bounds the predicate *before* the run, when both failure directions are still cheap. Use PAT-138 when a reading already exists and is doubted; use this one when the predicate is still being written.

## Consequences
- **Benefits**: Stops an audit from degrading a correct corpus to satisfy its own exit condition · surfaces the claim-classes a token list can never reach · converts an unsatisfiable criterion into a dispositionable one · makes correct-by-scoping cases visible instead of adversarial.
- **Tradeoffs**: Two enumerations cost real time up front, and the homonym list is the one people skip · "every hit fixed or classified" produces a longer record than a single zero.
- **Risks**: The allow-list can be used to wave through genuine defects — a classification is a claim and needs its own one-line justification, or it becomes a second silent failure.

## Examples

### Before/After Comparison

**Without Pattern**: A truth-pass ships eleven grep-shaped detectors and closes on "the dead-token set returns 0 hits". Four guides pass while asserting false behaviour in plain prose, and the exit criterion collides with three live constructs whose only literal satisfaction is to edit correct files into wrong ones.

**With Pattern**: The same eleven detectors ship alongside a no-grep companion scoped to the whole artifact and an allow-list naming `mcp_tool`, `mcpServers`, `85%` (pattern-effectiveness maturity) and `{placeholder}` (command-template parameter). The criterion becomes "every hit is fixed or classified", the four prose-only false claims are caught by the companion, and no correct file is touched.

### Application Example

A step's verify condition read "dead-token grep over the 5 live files returns 0". At the gate the condition was tested rather than executed: four of the five named files were **correct as written**, and satisfying the condition literally required corrupting them. The step was reduced from five files to one and the criterion rewritten as a disposition rule — a rule amendment ruled at a gate, not an override.

## Validation
- **Success Indicator**: The audit's record contains a homonym allow-list with a one-line justification per entry, at least one detector with no grep behind it, and a consumed-rows receipt per judgment-shaped detector.
- **Edge Cases**: Where the corpus is authored by many hands over years, the homonym list is never complete — treat the first unsatisfiable hit as a signal to amend the predicate at a gate rather than as an exception to wave through.

## Notes and Observations

The two directions have different tells. **Too narrow** announces itself as a suspiciously easy pass — an audit that finds nothing in a corpus nobody believes is clean. **Too broad** announces itself as friction: correct work keeps failing the criterion, and the temptation is to "fix" the work rather than the criterion. Treat both as predicate defects until proven otherwise, because in both cases the corpus is the newer, better-reviewed artifact and the predicate is the assumption-laden one.
